Why this order?You cannot overlap what you have not named, and you cannot trust a speed-up you did not measure against a recorded before picture. The runner that keeps answers under their keys comes before the plan, because the plan hands its calls to that runner. The pace comes after the failures, because a 429 is one of the failures you must be able to see first. The byte-for-byte proof comes last, because it needs every other piece in place.

Thread pool, process pool or asyncio?

  

  
The rule that decides itIf the time goes to waiting, overlap the waits with threads (blocking code) or asyncio (code written with await). If the time goes to computing, only more processes help, and only when each item carries a lot of work. If you have not measured, measure first.

Choosing the tool

  

  

    
Where does the time go?ToolNote

    
Waiting, with blocking codeThreadPoolExecutorA timeout stops the waiting, not the thread. Eight waits of 0.2 s: 1.60 s became 0.40 s on four threads

    
Waiting, with async defasyncio.gather, asyncio.timeout, SemaphoreNever block inside a coroutine: five blocking sleeps took 1.00 s, not 0.20 s

    
Computing, with large work per itemProcessPoolExecutorStart-up and pickling cost: 0.043 s against 0.007 s for tiny jobs, 3.515 s against 6.861 s at 100,000 rounds

    
Computing, small items, or local filesOne processThreads took 0.31 s on local files either way

SettingWhat it boundsClass value

[sources] workersHow many source pulls run together3. The fallback is 1
